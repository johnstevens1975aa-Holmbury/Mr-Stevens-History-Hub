What is History?

How do historians know what happened?

Knowledge spine

Students build the disciplinary toolkit before moving into Norman England. The sequence covers chronology, BC/AD where BC counts backwards, primary and secondary sources, source usefulness, bias, fact/opinion and cause/consequence. WACA is taught as Who, Audience, Context and Aim, with students judging usefulness and limitations rather than simply deciding whether a source is true or false.

Lesson route

  1. Chronology and timelines: place 10,000 BC, 1066, 1348, 1807, 1914 and modern events in order.
  2. Primary and secondary sources: classify Bayeux Tapestry, Chronicle, diary, photograph, textbook and documentary examples.
  3. Fact, opinion, cause and consequence: sort statements and explain why historians separate evidence from interpretation.
  4. WACA practice: evaluate a source and produce a balanced usefulness judgement.

Classroom tasks

  • Timeline challenge
  • Source classification grid
  • Bayeux Tapestry WACA
  • Fact/opinion/cause/effect sort
  • History Dinner Party evidence task

Vocabulary

ChronologyBC/ADPrimary sourceSecondary sourceProvenanceBiasFactOpinionCauseConsequenceWACA

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Primary sources are not automatically reliable.
  • Bias does not make a source useless.
  • BC years count backwards.
  • One event can have several causes and consequences.

Assessment route

Write a WACA paragraph explaining how useful one source is for a historian studying 1066.

Norman Conquest 1066

Why did William win control of England?

Knowledge spine

Edward the Confessor died on 5 January 1066 without a clear heir. England, with a population of roughly 1.5 million, faced a succession crisis. Harold was chosen by the Witan and crowned immediately, but William claimed an earlier promise and oath, Hardrada claimed through a Viking treaty, and Edgar had royal blood but little power. The year moved through Fulford, Stamford Bridge and Hastings before William forced submission at Wallingford and Berkhamsted and was crowned on Christmas Day 1066. Long-term consequences included Domesday, castles, feudal landholding, Church reform and thousands of French words entering English.

Lesson route

  1. England in January 1066: Edward’s death, Witan, wealth, claimants and instability.
  2. Four claimants: Harold, William, Hardrada and Edgar compared for legal and military strength.
  3. Three battles: Fulford, Stamford Bridge and Hastings, including shield wall, cavalry, archers and possible feigned retreat.
  4. After Hastings: William’s march round London, coronation, Harrying of the North and long-term transformation.

Classroom tasks

  • Claimant Top Trumps
  • Legal vs military claim ranking
  • Battle evidence board
  • Bayeux Tapestry WACA
  • William’s route map
  • 16-mark judgement on the most important consequence

Vocabulary

Edward the ConfessorWitanHarold GodwinsonHarald HardradaWilliam of NormandyEdgar ÆthelingFulfordStamford BridgeHastingsDomesdayFeudal system

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Hastings did not instantly secure England.
  • William’s victory had several causes, not one simple reason.
  • The Bayeux Tapestry is useful but not neutral.
  • Edgar had blood claim but lacked support and military power.

Assessment route

Explain why Harold lost Hastings and judge whether military skill, Harold’s mistakes or luck mattered most.

Medieval Entertainment and Life

What does leisure reveal about medieval society?

Knowledge spine

Medieval leisure was live, communal and shaped by holy days. Mob football involved whole communities, pig’s-bladder balls, Shrove Tuesday games and repeated bans from 1314 to 1667. Sources such as William Fitzstephen c.1174 and hostile church comments allow WACA evaluation. Minstrels spread songs, stories and news; mystery plays appeared in many towns; sport, music, fashion and diet all reveal hierarchy. Nobles displayed status through bright cloth, fur, spices and feasts, while peasants relied on bread, pottage, fish and weak ale. English also changed after 1066, with French and Latin dominant in elite life.

Lesson route

  1. Holy days, fairs, games and community leisure.
  2. Mob football: violence, popularity, royal bans and source evaluation.
  3. Fashion, food and sumptuary laws: class, status and social control.
  4. Language after 1066: Anglo-Saxon, Norse and Norman French influences.

Classroom tasks

  • Football court case
  • Minstrel news report
  • Class feast comparison
  • Sumptuary law investigation
  • Story of English word sort

Vocabulary

Holy dayMob footballMinstrelMystery playSumptuary lawsPottagePoulainesNorman FrenchChaucerSocial hierarchy

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Medieval life was not only work and suffering.
  • Repeated bans show royal power had limits.
  • Peasant life was not culturally empty.
  • Class affected access, but some leisure crossed boundaries.

Assessment route

Use evidence to explain how medieval entertainment reflected both shared culture and social inequality.

Health, Medicine and the Black Death

Why was the Black Death devastating and how did people respond?

Knowledge spine

The Black Death spread from Central Asia to the Black Sea, Italy, France, Weymouth in June 1348, London in autumn 1348 and across Britain and northern Europe by 1350. Bubonic plague spread through fleas and rats, with 30–70% mortality; pneumonic forms could kill within days. People explained the plague through God’s punishment, miasma, astrology, humours and scapegoating, while responses included prayer, flight, pomanders, flagellants, bloodletting and isolation. Its consequences were huge: population collapse, labour shortages, wage pressure, the Statute of Labourers 1351 and conditions that helped lead to the Peasants’ Revolt of 1381.

Lesson route

  1. Spread and symptoms: mapping plague movement and comparing bubonic/pneumonic plague.
  2. Beliefs and treatments: religion, miasma, humours, astrology, scapegoating and practical isolation.
  3. Sources and images: Danse Macabre, John of Reading and WACA practice.
  4. Consequences: labour shortage, wages, Statute of Labourers and Peasants’ Revolt.

Classroom tasks

  • Plague route map
  • Treatment ranking
  • Physician/apothecary/barber-surgeon comparison
  • Danse Macabre WACA
  • Consequence chain

Vocabulary

Yersinia pestisBuboesMiasmaFour HumoursFlagellantQuarantineBloodlettingStatute of LabourersPeasants’ Revolt

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Medieval remedies were not all random; some fitted the beliefs of the time.
  • Religious explanations were part of a wider worldview.
  • The Black Death changed society, not only population numbers.

Assessment route

Judge whether the most important consequence of the Black Death was economic, social or political.

Castles: Power and Defence

Were castles built for fighting, fear or control?

Knowledge spine

Norman castles controlled England after 1066. Motte-and-bailey castles could be built quickly, sometimes within days, and over 500 were built in the decades after conquest. They intimidated, housed soldiers, controlled roads and displayed lordship. Stone keeps from c.1070 improved durability, with gatehouses, moats, battlements and murder holes. Rochester 1215 illustrates siege engineering: King John ordered forty fat pigs so burning fat could collapse a mined tower. Castles declined as gunpowder and stronger royal authority made private fortresses less useful.

Lesson route

  1. Why castles were built: control, intimidation, local power and defence.
  2. Motte-and-bailey design: speed, strengths, weaknesses and site selection.
  3. Stone keeps and siege warfare: Tower of London, Rochester and military engineering.
  4. Concentric castles, decline and independent research project.

Classroom tasks

  • Choose the best castle site
  • Label a castle diagram
  • Rochester siege decision task
  • Castle decline debate
  • Research project with WACA and PEEL

Vocabulary

MotteBaileyKeepPalisadePortcullisTrebuchetConcentricSiegeRochesterGunpowder

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Simple wooden castles were not useless.
  • Castles were political symbols as well as military buildings.
  • Sieges were about engineering and supplies, not just fighting.

Assessment route

Explain whether castles helped William control England more through fear, defence or administration.

Transatlantic Slave Trade, Resistance and Abolition

How did slavery shape empire, economy and resistance?

Knowledge spine

The unit begins with West African kingdoms such as Ghana, Mali, Songhay, Benin and Timbuktu to challenge a single-story narrative. It then traces the transatlantic slave trade from c.1500–1900, with about 12.5 million Africans transported. Students examine triangular trade, the Middle Passage of 6–8 weeks, the Brookes Diagram, Equiano, Mary Prince, plantation labour, family separation, resistance including Haiti 1791, abolition campaigns and modern slavery. The sequence stresses agency, resistance and legacy rather than only victimhood.

Lesson route

  1. West Africa before slavery: kingdoms, trade, scholarship and cultural life.
  2. Triangular Trade and Middle Passage: profit, ships, mortality and evidence.
  3. Plantations, resistance and abolitionist sources.
  4. Abolition, compensation, memory, modern slavery and responsibility.

Classroom tasks

  • Challenge the single-story task
  • Brookes Diagram WACA
  • Middle Passage evidence diary
  • Abolition campaign plan
  • Legacy debate

Vocabulary

GhanaMaliSonghayBeninTriangular TradeMiddle PassageBrookesEquianoWilberforceAbolitionModern slavery

Misconceptions to tackle

  • African history does not begin with enslavement.
  • Abolition was not achieved by one politician alone.
  • Enslaved people resisted and shaped events.
  • Legal abolition did not end exploitation or racism.

Assessment route

Assess whether abolition happened mainly because of resistance, campaigners, economics or politics.

Farms to Factories

How did industrialisation transform work and society?

Knowledge spine

Industrial Britain shifted from domestic production to factory work between 1750 and 1900. Inventions included the Flying Shuttle 1733, Spinning Jenny 1764, Water Frame 1769, Spinning Mule 1779 and Power Loom 1785. Arkwright’s Cromford Mill, 1771, models the factory system. Population growth, steam power, raw materials, empire and transport all mattered. Students study Bradford and Manchester growth, child labour, factory discipline, the Sadler Report and Factory Acts of 1833, 1844 and 1847.

Lesson route

  1. Why Britain industrialised: resources, population, transport, empire and invention.
  2. Domestic system to factory system: technology, discipline and profit.
  3. Urbanisation: Bradford and Manchester as case studies.
  4. Child labour and reform: Sadler, inspectors and Factory Acts.

Classroom tasks

  • Invention consequence chain
  • Bradford graph
  • Factory inspector report
  • Child labour source WACA
  • Change-and-continuity paragraph

Vocabulary

Domestic systemFactory systemFlying ShuttleSpinning JennyWater FramePower LoomUrbanisationChild labourFactory Acts

Misconceptions to tackle

  • One invention did not cause the Industrial Revolution alone.
  • Factories brought both opportunity and exploitation.
  • Reform was gradual and contested.

Assessment route

Explain the most significant impact of the Industrial Revolution on ordinary people.

Victorian Living Conditions and Public Health

Why were Victorian towns dangerous and how did public health improve?

Knowledge spine

Victorian towns were crowded, polluted and poorly drained. Manchester life expectancy was around 28 in 1850, and cholera, typhoid and TB spread through dirty water, overcrowding and poor ventilation. Chadwick’s 1842 report argued for sanitary reform, while John Snow’s Broad Street pump investigation of 1854 challenged miasma thinking. The Great Stink of 1858 helped push sewer reform under Bazalgette. Public Health Acts in 1848 and especially 1875 show growing government responsibility for sanitation.

Lesson route

  1. Victorian towns: overcrowding, sewage, housing and industrial pollution.
  2. Disease comparison: cholera, typhoid and TB.
  3. Source analysis: Chadwick, John Snow and health inspector evidence.
  4. Reform: Great Stink, Bazalgette and Public Health Acts.

Classroom tasks

  • Health Inspector case file
  • Disease comparison chart
  • Observe-Infer-Prove-Reflect source routine
  • Public health poster
  • Greatest threat judgement

Vocabulary

CholeraTyphoidTBMiasmaGerm TheoryChadwickJohn SnowBazalgetteGreat StinkPublic Health Act

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Victorians were not simply ignorant; they worked with available evidence.
  • Miasma made sense before Germ Theory was accepted.
  • Government action often followed pressure, crisis and evidence.

Assessment route

Judge whether evidence, government action or crisis was most important in improving public health.

British Empire and India

How should the British Empire be interpreted?

Knowledge spine

By 1900 the British Empire ruled about a quarter of the world’s land and around 400 million people. Students explore motives: raw materials, markets, naval bases, prestige, trade and power. India is studied as the “Jewel in the Crown”: East India Company from 1600, Plassey 1757, Rebellion 1857, direct Raj, Victoria as Empress 1877. The unit weighs railways, English education and infrastructure against extraction, famine, textile decline, racism and violence, using sources from Mangal Pandey, Dalhousie and Macaulay.

Lesson route

  1. Why empire expanded: trade, conquest, prestige, settlement and strategy.
  2. India before and during Company rule: Mughal India, EIC and Plassey.
  3. Rebellion and Raj: 1857, direct rule and imperial attitudes.
  4. Legacy and interpretation: benefits, exploitation, nationalism and apology debate.

Classroom tasks

  • Empire balance sheet
  • Macaulay/Dalhousie WACA
  • Indian vs British headline task
  • Apology debate
  • Interpretation essay

Vocabulary

EmpireColonyEast India CompanyPlasseySepoyRajMacaulayDalhousieGandhiNehru

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Empire was not a simple story of progress or evil.
  • Railways and education can be both useful and exploitative.
  • Perspectives differ depending on who benefited and who paid the cost.

Assessment route

Evaluate whether British rule in India did more harm than good, using evidence from both sides.

Nelson and Trafalgar

Why did Trafalgar matter and how should Nelson be remembered?

Knowledge spine

Britain relied on naval supremacy in 1805. The Royal Navy had over 830 warships and 140,000 sailors; HMS Victory had 104 guns and a crew of about 820. Students study life at sea, impressment, prize money, discipline, scurvy prevention and Nelson’s leadership. Trafalgar on 21 October 1805 involved 27 British ships against 33 Franco-Spanish ships. Nelson’s two-column tactic broke the line; he was shot around 1:15pm and died about 4:30pm. Eighteen enemy ships were captured and none British were lost. The legacy debate covers heroism, empire, memory and controversy.

Lesson route

  1. Britain’s naval world: invasion threat, sailors, HMS Victory and life at sea.
  2. Nelson’s career and leadership: Nile, Copenhagen, Nelson Touch, recklessness vs calculation.
  3. Trafalgar: two-column tactic, timeline, outcomes and source evidence.
  4. Legacy and memory: Pax Britannica, Nelson’s Column, empire and controversy.

Classroom tasks

  • HMS Victory evidence board
  • Nelson memorandum WACA
  • Trafalgar tactical map
  • Museum guide assessment
  • Commemoration debate

Vocabulary

Royal NavyHMS VictoryImpressmentPrize moneyNelson TouchBroadsideBlockadeTrafalgarPax Britannica

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Trafalgar did not end the Napoleonic Wars.
  • Nelson’s heroism is an interpretation, not a simple fact.
  • Leadership, tactics, training, luck and naval power all mattered.

Assessment route

Judge whether Nelson, tactics, sailors or wider British naval power best explains victory at Trafalgar.

Year 9 · WW1.pptx · 51 slides mapped

World War One

Why did war break out and how did total war change lives?

Knowledge spine

The First World War is taught through long-term M.A.I.N. causes and the Sarajevo spark. Students track the July Crisis from 28 June to Britain’s entry on 4 August 1914. Trench warfare developed after the Marne and extended roughly 700km. The Somme’s first day caused 57,470 British casualties; Verdun and Passchendaele show attrition. The unit includes weapons, medicine, shell shock, Gillies, the Home Front, DORA, women’s work, conscription, propaganda, Armistice and the Treaty of Versailles.

Lesson route

  1. M.A.I.N. causes and Sarajevo: causation wheel and historian interpretations.
  2. Trenches and industrial warfare: stalemate, Somme, Verdun, weapons and medicine.
  3. Home Front: DORA, women, propaganda, conscription and total war.
  4. End of war: Armistice, Versailles, B-R-A-T and remembrance.

Classroom tasks

  • MAIN carousel
  • Trench letter WACA
  • Weapon impact ranking
  • Home Front role cards
  • Versailles judgement essay

Vocabulary

MilitarismAlliancesImperialismNationalismSarajevoSchlieffen PlanTrenchesSommeDORAConscription

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Sarajevo was the spark, not the only cause.
  • Trenches were not identical everywhere.
  • New technology caused stalemate but also forced adaptation.
  • The Armistice and Treaty of Versailles are different events.

Assessment route

Evaluate whether long-term causes or the Sarajevo crisis mattered more in causing the First World War.

Treaty of Versailles

Was the Treaty of Versailles fair, harsh or badly enforced?

Knowledge spine

The Armistice of 11 November 1918 ended the fighting; the Treaty was made at the Paris Peace Conference from 18 January 1919. Wilson, Lloyd George and Clemenceau wanted different outcomes. Germany was excluded. Terms included Article 231 War Guilt, reparations of £6.6 billion, territorial losses, colonial losses, army limited to 100,000, no air force or submarines and a demilitarised Rhineland. The unit evaluates interpretations such as Keynes and MacMillan and links the Treaty to the League of Nations.

Lesson route

  1. Armistice vs Treaty: context, Big Three aims and Germany’s exclusion.
  2. BRAT terms: blame, reparations, army restrictions and territory.
  3. Interpretations: Keynes, MacMillan and debate over harshness.
  4. Consequences: German resentment, League creation and enforcement problems.

Classroom tasks

  • Big Three roleplay
  • BRAT organiser
  • Keynes source WACA
  • Fairness continuum
  • 16-mark judgement plan

Vocabulary

ArmisticeParis Peace ConferenceBig ThreeArticle 231ReparationsDiktatBRATLeague of Nations

Misconceptions to tackle

  • The Armistice was not the Treaty.
  • Reparations were one part of a wider settlement.
  • A treaty can be harsh but still poorly enforced.

Assessment route

How far do you agree that the Treaty of Versailles was too harsh on Germany?

League of Nations

Why did the League struggle to keep peace?

Knowledge spine

The League grew from Wilson’s Fourteen Points and collective security. Its structure included the Assembly, Council, Court and commissions such as Health, ILO, Refugee and Slavery. It had successes in the 1920s: Aaland Islands, Upper Silesia, Locarno, Germany joining and humanitarian work including 400,000 POWs repatriated. Weaknesses included USA absence, no army, unanimity and reliance on Britain and France. The Depression and aggressive nationalism exposed these issues through Manchuria, Abyssinia, Hoare-Laval and Rhineland.

Lesson route

  1. Origins, aims and structure: Wilson, collective security, Assembly, Council and commissions.
  2. 1920s successes and humanitarian work.
  3. 1930s failures: Manchuria, Abyssinia, Hoare-Laval, Rhineland.
  4. Why the League failed: USA, self-interest, Depression, no army, democratic reluctance and legacy.

Classroom tasks

  • League structure diagram
  • Success/failure sorting
  • Selassie WACA
  • Failure-factor ranking
  • Abyssinia “death of the League” debate

Vocabulary

Collective securityCovenantSanctionsUnanimityAalandUpper SilesiaManchuriaAbyssiniaHoare-LavalRhineland

Misconceptions to tackle

  • The League did not fail at everything.
  • Manchuria, Abyssinia and Rhineland exposed different weaknesses.
  • Collective security only works if states enforce it.

Assessment route

Judge whether the League failed mainly because of its structure or because member states refused to act.

Cold War 1943–72

Why did superpower rivalry divide the world?

Knowledge spine

The Cold War grew from ideological rivalry, security fears and the breakdown of the Grand Alliance. Students compare Tehran, Yalta and Potsdam; the atomic bomb; Kennan’s Long Telegram; and Churchill’s Iron Curtain speech. Containment is studied through the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, Cominform and Berlin Blockade/Airlift. Flashpoints include Korea, Hungary, Berlin Wall, Cuba and Prague Spring, ending with détente measures such as the Hot Line, Test Ban Treaty, Non-Proliferation Treaty and SALT I.

Lesson route

  1. Origins: ideology, conferences, atomic diplomacy and source utility.
  2. Containment and Berlin: Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, blockade and airlift.
  3. Rivalry and crisis: Korea, Hungary, Berlin Wall and Cuba.
  4. Détente: Prague Spring, Brezhnev Doctrine, SALT I and limits.

Classroom tasks

  • Conference comparison
  • Ideology briefing
  • Berlin decision task
  • Cuba crisis room
  • Edexcel 6/8/16-mark practice

Vocabulary

CapitalismCommunismYaltaPotsdamContainmentTruman DoctrineMarshall PlanBerlin AirliftMADDétente

Misconceptions to tackle

  • The Cold War was not a conventional direct war between USA and USSR.
  • Containment was political, economic and military.
  • Détente reduced tension but did not end rivalry.

Assessment route

How far do you agree that Soviet expansionism was the main cause of the Cold War?

Stalin’s Russia 1924–53

How far did Stalin transform Russia through policy, propaganda and terror?

Knowledge spine

Stalin’s Russia covers the power struggle from 1924–29, suppression of Lenin’s Testament, General Secretary power and defeat of rivals. The Five-Year Plans and collectivisation created rapid industrial growth, including steel from 4m to 18m tonnes, but at huge human cost, including Holodomor and Gulag labour. The Great Purge of 1936–38 saw about 1.5 million arrested and 750,000 executed. Students also study propaganda, censorship, education, social change, WW2, Stalingrad and Stalin’s contested legacy.

Lesson route

  1. Rise to power: Party machine, alliances, rivals and ideology.
  2. Economy: Five-Year Plans, collectivisation, industrialisation and human cost.
  3. Terror and control: NKVD, show trials, Gulag, purges and propaganda.
  4. Society, war and legacy: living standards, women, education, WW2 and judgement.

Classroom tasks

  • Rival card sort
  • Industrial data analysis
  • Purge source evaluation
  • Terror vs propaganda debate
  • 16-mark balanced judgement

Vocabulary

Lenin’s TestamentGeneral SecretaryFive-Year PlansCollectivisationHolodomorGulagNKVDGreat PurgeCult of PersonalityStalingrad

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Industrial success and human cost must both be addressed.
  • Propaganda and terror worked together.
  • Stalin’s WW2 legacy is contested.

Assessment route

How far did Stalin’s economic policies benefit the Soviet Union?

iGCSE · Vietnam.pptx · 120 slides mapped

Vietnam War 1945–75

Why did the USA fail in Vietnam?

Knowledge spine

The Vietnam course begins with French rule, Japanese occupation, Ho Chi Minh’s independence declaration, the First Indochina War, Dien Bien Phu and Geneva 1954. US escalation is linked to Domino Theory, Diem, the NLF, Gulf of Tonkin, Rolling Thunder, Search and Destroy, Agent Orange and strategic hamlets. Vietcong guerrilla tactics, tunnels and Ho Chi Minh Trail made victory difficult. Tet 1968 was a military defeat for the NLF but a political turning point. Withdrawal, protest, Vietnamisation, Paris 1973 and Fall of Saigon 1975 complete the story.

Lesson route

  1. Origins: French rule, Ho Chi Minh, First Indochina War, Dien Bien Phu and Geneva.
  2. US escalation: Domino Theory, Diem, Tonkin, Rolling Thunder and troop buildup.
  3. Fighting the war: Vietcong tactics, tunnels, Ho Chi Minh Trail and US tactics.
  4. Tet, protest and withdrawal: media, My Lai, Kent State, Vietnamisation and Saigon.

Classroom tasks

Changes in Medicine c.1848–1948

Why did medicine change between 1848 and 1948?

Knowledge spine

Medicine begins with miasma, humours, unsafe surgery, no antibiotics and limited statistics. Early improvements include anaesthetics, vaccination, Chadwick, Snow and Nightingale.   3. Government and war: public health, Liberal Reforms, WW1 and WW2 medicine.
  4. Penicillin, NHS and five factors: chance, individuals, government, war and technology.

What is History?

How do historians know what happened?

Knowledge spine

Students build the disciplinary toolkit before moving into Norman England. The sequence covers chronology, BC/AD where BC counts backwards, primary and secondary sources, source usefulness, bias, fact/opinion and cause/consequence.   3. Fact, opinion, cause and consequence: sort statements and explain why historians separate evidence from interpretation.
  4. WACA practice: evaluate a source and produce a balanced usefulness judgement.

Classroom tasks

  • Timeline challenge
  • Source classification grid
  • Bayeux Tapestry WACA
  • Fact/opinion/cause/effect sort
  • History Dinner Party evidence task

ChronologyBC/ADPrimary sourceSecondary sourceProvenanceBiasFactOpinionCauseConsequenceWACA

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Primary sources are not automatically reliable.
  • Bias does not make a source useless.
  • BC years count backwards.
  • One event can have several causes and consequences.

Norman Conquest 1066

Why did William win control of England?

Knowledge spine

Edward the Confessor died on 5 January 1066 without a clear heir. England, with a population of roughly 1.5 million, faced a succession crisis. Harold was chosen by the Witan and crowned immediately, but William claimed an earlier promise and oath, Hardrada claimed through a Viking treaty, and Edgar had royal blood but little power. The year moved through Fulford, Stamford Bridge and Hastings before William forced submission at Wallingford and Berkhamsted and was crowned on Christmas Day 1066. Long-term consequences included Domesday, castles, feudal landholding, Church reform and thousands of French words entering English.

  1. England in January 1066: Edward’s death, Witan, wealth, claimants and instability.

  3. Three battles: Fulford, Stamford Bridge and Hastings, including shield wall, cavalry, archers and possible feigned retreat.
  4. After Hastings: William’s march round London, coronation, Harrying of the North and long-term transformation.

  • Legal vs military claim ranking
  • Battle evidence board
  • Bayeux Tapestry WACA
  • William’s route map
  • 16-mark judgement on the most important consequence

Medieval Entertainment and Life

What does leisure reveal about medieval society?

Knowledge spine

Medieval leisure was live, communal and shaped by holy days. Mob football involved whole communities, pig’s-bladder balls, Shrove Tuesday games and repeated bans from 1314 to 1667. Minstrels spread songs, stories and news; mystery plays appeared in many towns; sport, music, fashion and diet all reveal hierarchy. Health, Medicine and the Black Death

Why was the Black Death devastating and how did people respond?

Knowledge spine

The Black Death spread from Central Asia to the Black Sea, Italy, France, Weymouth in June 1348, London in autumn 1348 and across Britain and northern Europe by 1350. Bubonic plague spread through fleas and rats, with 30–70% mortality; pneumonic forms could kill within days. People explained the plague through God’s punishment, miasma, astrology, humours and scapegoating, while responses included prayer, flight, pomanders, flagellants, bloodletting and isolation. Its consequences were huge: population collapse, labour shortages, wage pressure, the Statute of Labourers 1351 and conditions that helped lead to the Peasants’ Revolt of 1381.

  4. Consequences: labour shortage, wages, Statute of Labourers and Peasants’ Revolt.

Castles: Power and Defence

Were castles built for fighting, fear or control?

Knowledge spine

Norman castles controlled England after 1066. Motte-and-bailey castles could be built quickly, sometimes within days, and over 500 were built in the decades after conquest.   4. Concentric castles, decline and independent research project.

Victorian Living Conditions and Public Health

Why were Victorian towns dangerous and how did public health improve?

Knowledge spine

Victorian towns were crowded, polluted and poorly drained. Manchester life expectancy was around 28 in 1850, and cholera, typhoid and TB spread through dirty water, overcrowding and poor ventilation. Why did the USA fail in Vietnam?

Knowledge spine

The Vietnam course begins with French rule, Japanese occupation, Ho Chi Minh’s independence declaration, the First Indochina War, Dien Bien Phu and Geneva 1954. US escalation is linked to Domino Theory, Diem, the NLF, Gulf of Tonkin, Rolling Thunder, Search and Destroy, Agent Orange and strategic hamlets. Vietcong guerrilla tactics, tunnels and Ho Chi Minh Trail made victory difficult. Tet 1968 was a military defeat for the NLF but a political turning point. Withdrawal, protest, Vietnamisation, Paris 1973 and Fall of Saigon 1975 complete the story.

Lesson route

  1. Origins: French rule, Ho Chi Minh, First Indochina War, Dien Bien Phu and Geneva.
  2. US escalation: Domino Theory, Diem, Tonkin, Rolling Thunder and troop buildup.
  3. Fighting the war: Vietcong tactics, tunnels, Ho Chi Minh Trail and US tactics.
  4. Tet, protest and withdrawal: media, My Lai, Kent State, Vietnamisation and Saigon.

Classroom tasks

  • War correspondent report
  • Tactics comparison
  • McNamara WACA
  • Tet media analysis
  • US failure 16-mark plan

Changes in Medicine c.1848–1948

Why did medicine change between 1848 and 1948?

Knowledge spine

Medicine begins with miasma, humours, unsafe surgery, no antibiotics and limited statistics. Early improvements include anaesthetics, vaccination, Chadwick, Snow and Nightingale. Germ Theory transformed understanding after Pasteur 1861; Lister applied antiseptic surgery in 1867; Koch identified anthrax, TB and cholera; Ehrlich developed Salvarsan 606 in 1909. Public health reform involved 1848 and 1875 Acts, Great Stink and Liberal Reforms. Wars accelerated transfusions, X-rays, plastic surgery and penicillin mass production. Beveridge and Bevan led to the NHS in 1948.

Lesson route

  1. Medicine c.1848: old ideas, miasma, anaesthetics, sanitation and limitations.
  2. Germ Theory: Pasteur, Lister, Koch, Ehrlich and scientific medicine.
  3. Government and war: public health, Liberal Reforms, WW1 and WW2 medicine.
  4. Penicillin, NHS and five factors: chance, individuals, government, war and technology.

Classroom tasks

  • Medicine factor tournament
  • Lister WACA
  • Public health timeline
  • Penicillin 12-year gap debate
  • NHS significance essay

Vocabulary

MiasmaGerm TheoryPasteurListerKochSalvarsan 606Public Health ActPenicillinBeveridgeNHS

Misconceptions to tackle

  • Fleming did not create usable penicillin alone.
  • War accelerated progress but did not invent every advance.
  • Public health depended on science, government, pressure and money.

Assessment route

Which factor was most important in medical progress: individuals, government, war, technology or chance?

Source Studies

Bayeux Tapestry, c.1077

Bishop Odo commission, Hastings narrative, useful but shaped by Norman power.

Anglo-Saxon Chronicle, c.1100

Monastic hindsight on 1066, useful for English perspective but written after events.

Brookes Slave Ship Diagram, 1787

Abolitionist visual evidence of Middle Passage conditions and campaign purpose.

Chadwick Report, 1842

Government reform source, miasma context and sanitary argument.

WW1 Trench Letter, 1916

Censored soldier evidence of mud, exhaustion and emotional limitation.

Haile Selassie Speech, June 1936

Appeal to League Assembly after Abyssinia, source for collective security failure.

Churchill Iron Curtain Speech, 1946

Cold War source that both describes and helps shape divided Europe.

CIA Cuba Photograph, 1962

Visual intelligence source, strong on physical evidence but limited on intention.

Lenin’s Testament, 1923

Warning about Stalin, useful for power struggle but suppressed and politically sensitive.

Ho Chi Minh Declaration, 1945

Nationalist/anti-colonial appeal quoting US independence language.

Joseph Lister, The Lancet, 1867

Antiseptic surgery source applying Pasteur’s Germ Theory.

Aneurin Bevan NHS Speech, 1946

Political argument for healthcare regardless of means.
